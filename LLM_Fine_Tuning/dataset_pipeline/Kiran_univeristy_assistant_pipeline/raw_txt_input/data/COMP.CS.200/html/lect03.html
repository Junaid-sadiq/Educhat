Image file types

  • gif limited color palette, best for small, simple animations (lossless). File size can be large for complex animations.
  • jpg offers good compression for photos, but loses quality with each save (lossy). Not ideal for sharp graphics or images with text.
  • png supports transparency, making it suitable for logos and non-rectangular images (lossless). File size can be larger than jpg.
  • webp modern format developed by Google (lossy/lossless). Offers superior compression and quality compared to jpg. Not all browsers support webp yet, so consider offering a fallback format.

Resolution

The term "resolution" has two main meanings related to images and displays:

  1. image detail (pixels-per-inch PPI): This refers to the level of detail or sharpness of an image, especially when printed. It's measured in pixels per inch (ppi). Higher ppi values generally indicate sharper images.
    • Printing: for reasonable print quality, a resolution of around 256 ppi is considered acceptable.
    • Displays: typical displays have resolutions ranging from 72 to 100 ppi due to the viewing distance.
  2. image dimensions (pixels): In everyday terms, resolution often refers to the image size, expressed as the number of pixels in width and height.
    • example: an 8-megapixel camera might capture photos with a resolution of 3840 x 2160 pixels

    impact of resolution

    • on screen: increasing resolution typically only enlarges the image without necessarily improving sharpness
    • in print: increasing resolution can enhance detail and sharpness (up to a certain point), but it can also increase the printed size

Browser Support for Video Formats

Early days of web video saw inconsistencies in browser support for video formats (think Apple QuickTime vs. RealPlayer). This meant requiring users to download specific codecs or plugins to play videos, creating a fragmented viewing experience.

Today, ensuring compatibility across browsers requires a multi-format approach:

  • multiple video formats: videos are often encoded in at least two formats to cater to a wider range of browsers. Popular choices include:
    • MP4 (H.264 + AAC): widely supported and efficient, but may have licensing restrictions.
    • WebM (VP8/VP9 + Vorbis/Opus): open-source and royalty-free, with strong support in modern browsers.
    • Ogg (Theora + Vorbis): Another open-source option, sometimes used in conjunction with WebM.
  • Browsers often leverage video codecs built into the operating system or hardware for playback. This simplifies content embedding from services like YouTube or Maps, which often provide ready-to-use snippets with multiple video formats.

Providing several formats
