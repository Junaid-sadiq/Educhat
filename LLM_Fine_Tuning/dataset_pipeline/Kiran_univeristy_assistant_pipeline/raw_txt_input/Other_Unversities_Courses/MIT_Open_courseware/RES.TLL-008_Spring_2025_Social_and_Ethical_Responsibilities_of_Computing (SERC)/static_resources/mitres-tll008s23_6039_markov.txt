Now, many of its state transitions are stochastic in response to specific actions: Wash: If you perform the "wash" operation on any object, whether it's dirty, clean, or painted, it will end up clean with probability 0.9 and dirty otherwise. Paint: If you perform the "paint" operation on a clean object, it will become nicely painted with probability 0.8. With probability 0.1, the paint misses but the object stays clean, and also with probability 0.1, the machine dumps rusty dust all over the object and it becomes dirty. If you perform the "paint" operation on a painted object, it stays painted with probability 1.0. If you perform the "paint" operation on a dirty object, it stays dirty with probability 1.0. Eject: If you perform an "eject" operation on any object, the object comes out of the machine and this fun game is over. The object remains ejected regardless of any further action. In this problem, we use a two-dimensional "grid-world" with a robot in it; we have a state for every square on the grid, representing the robot's location. Our four possible actions are moving North, South, East or West (note that the robot cannot move off the board, so some states have fewer possible actions). The transitions are also substantially noisy; when commanding a move to a target state, there is a 50% probability of moving to the target state, and a probability of landing instead at one of the vertical or horizontal neighbor states of the target state. In most cases, will be four, but at the boundaries of our grid-world, and the available transitions will include only the neighboring squares that are within grid-world. We want to identify the best action that the robot can take in each state (i.e., the best policy), if it had a specific horizon of remaining moves.
